Plastering a listed building in Devon

Devon is full of listed cottages, farmhouses and townhouses, and most of them are cob, stone or lath and plaster. Replacing lime with gypsum on a listed building is usually both a technical mistake and a breach of the listing, and unauthorised work to a listed building is a criminal offence.

What the listing actually covers

This is the bit people get wrong. A listing covers the whole building, inside and out, not just the front elevation.

That includes the plaster. Original lime plaster, lath and plaster ceilings, cornices, and the render on the outside are all part of the listed fabric. Hacking off original lime plaster and replacing it with gypsum board is an alteration to a listed building.

It also covers anything within the curtilage, so outbuildings and walls can be caught by it too.

When you need consent

Listed building consent is needed for any work that affects the building's character as a building of special interest. In practice, for plastering, that usually means:

Replacing original lime plaster or render with a different material. Removing lath and plaster ceilings. Removing or altering cornices and mouldings. Rendering a wall that was not rendered, or removing render from one that was.

Straight like-for-like repair in the same materials is generally more straightforward, but the safe answer is always to ask the conservation officer first. They would far rather have a phone call before the work than a complaint afterwards.

The penalties are real

Carrying out unauthorised works to a listed building is a criminal offence, not a planning technicality. It can lead to prosecution, and the council can serve an enforcement notice requiring you to put it back.

Putting a gypsum-skimmed, cement-rendered cottage back to lime, at your own cost, on a deadline, is not a position anyone wants to be in.

And there is a quieter risk: it will come up when you sell. Buyers' solicitors ask about listed building consent, and works that were never consented can hold a sale up or knock the price down.

Why lime is usually required anyway

Even leaving the law out of it, the technical answer is the same.

A listed Devon cottage is almost always cob, stone or solid brick, with lath and plaster ceilings. Those walls have no damp proof course and they were never meant to have one. They manage water by letting it move through and evaporate.

Seal that up with gypsum inside and cement outside, and the moisture goes somewhere else, which is usually into the timber. Rotten wall plates and beam ends in a listed building are a considerably more expensive problem than plaster.

So the conservation officer asking for lime is not being awkward. They are asking for the thing that will not wreck the building.

Good to know

Common questions

Do I need listed building consent to re-plaster?
Often, yes, particularly if you are changing materials, removing original lime plaster, or taking down a lath and plaster ceiling. Like-for-like repair in matching materials is usually more straightforward. Ring the conservation officer before you start rather than after.
Can I use normal plaster in a listed building?
Usually not, and usually you should not want to. Gypsum seals a wall that needs to breathe, which pushes moisture into the timbers. It is also an alteration to listed fabric, which needs consent.
What happens if I plaster a listed building without consent?
Unauthorised work to a listed building is a criminal offence. The council can prosecute and can serve an enforcement notice requiring you to undo it at your own expense. It also surfaces when you try to sell, because buyers' solicitors ask.
Is lath and plaster protected?
Original lath and plaster ceilings are part of the listed fabric and removing them generally needs consent. They can often be repaired rather than replaced, which is usually the outcome the conservation officer will want.
